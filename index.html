This page is a research demonstrator, not a warning system. It is built entirely on the historical PHIVOLCS earthquake catalogue, and it can only describe patterns that catalogue contains. The record

A century of Philippine seismicity, and what it honestly supports.

PHIVOLCS has logged earthquakes along the archipelago since 1907. This study uses the whole retrievable archive: every event with a complete origin time, epicentre, depth and at least one reported magnitude.

Cleaning that archive is where most of the honest work sits. The four magnitude scales are almost never co-reported with moment magnitude, so no defensible conversion could be fitted from this catalogue alone. The working magnitude falls back to the first available scale in the order Mw, Ms, mb, ML, and the paper says so plainly rather than implying a homogeneous record.

The catalogue is also complete only from magnitude 4.0 upward, because that is where PHIVOLCS begins publishing. Each event additionally carries its distance to the nearest active fault, measured against fault lines mapped in QGIS. Two in five sit within 25 km of one.

Our approach

From a raw bulletin to a forecast the calendar cannot cheat.

Every step exists to stop the model learning something it would not know in advance.

02

03

Engineer

Build 27 features from the 14 days and 100 km before each mainshock, target excluded.

04

Maeda's method

18,338 records in. 166 forecast sequences out.

Every step below names the condition that removed what it removed. The counts are read from the pipeline's own audit tables, not typed into this page.

What declustering is for

An aftershock sequence is not a warning of anything. It is the crust settling after an earthquake that has already happened, and a model that treats those tremors as precursors will learn to forecast the past. Maeda's method separates the two by asking, for every event, whether an earlier and larger earthquake has already claimed it.

An event of magnitude Mpre draws a window around itself in space and in time. Anything inside that window and at least one magnitude unit smaller is dependent: part of that earthquake's own sequence rather than an independent event. The windows come from Equations 1 and 2 of Hirose et al. (2021), and they widen steeply with magnitude.

2,207 of the 17,667 cleaned records are dependent by this test, which is one event in eight. Those are the swarm members and aftershocks that would otherwise look like precursory activity.

Which magnitude is used

PHIVOLCS reports up to four magnitudes for one earthquake: ML, mb, Ms and Mw. They measure different things and saturate at different sizes, so one working value has to be adopted per event. The rule is a fixed preference applied without looking at the value, so the choice cannot bend toward a convenient number.

The inputs

Twenty-seven numbers, computed from the window and nothing else.

The first twenty-two follow Wang et al. (2023) as tabulated in the study methodology. The last five summarise the distance to the nearest active fault, which the data collection names as a selected parameter. Every one is computed from the 14 days and 100 km before the mainshock; the mainshock's own properties are excluded, being what is under forecast.

Mean |SHAP| is in magnitude units: a feature with 0.12 moves a typical forecast by about a tenth of a magnitude unit. Blank means the feature carries less than a thousandth of a unit of influence. The model does not choose which equations to use and discard the rest; it is given all twenty-seven and weighs them, and the column above is how much weight each one actually got.

Results

Real skill, and small. Both halves of that sentence matter.

Extra Trees, Random Forest and LSTM, the three forecasters the study objectives name, scored once on 33 sequences none of them had seen. Two beat the historical average. One does not.

Model RMSE MSE MAE R squared 95% CI for RMSE Skill

Skill is the percentage reduction in mean squared error against climatology, which is the historical mean magnitude of the training sequences. A negative value means the model is worse than quoting that average. These figures are read from the pipeline's leaderboard table at build time, so the page cannot drift from the results.

What the numbers say

An Extra Trees ensemble on 27 features cuts squared error by 11.4% against quoting the historical average magnitude. Of the sixteen forecasters compared in the paper, three beat that baseline and thirteen do not. The typical error is 0.55 of a magnitude unit, the difference between a tremor and a damaging event, and the confidence interval on the leader runs from 0.397 to 0.701 against the baseline's 0.427 to 0.742. Those intervals overlap almost completely. On 33 held-out sequences this is a weak result honestly reported, not a strong one.

The LSTM is in the objectives and behind the baseline

The recurrent network scores 0.599 against climatology's 0.589, a skill of minus 3.4%. It is shown here because the study objectives name it, and reporting only the models that worked would be the more serious failure. With 100 training sequences of ten timesteps there is nothing like enough data for a network to pay for its parameters, and that is the finding, not a bug to be tuned away.

Fault distance: no measurable difference, either way

Dropping the five fault features and refitting, under two protocols

Dropping the five fault features and refitting gives 0.547 against the 0.555 scored with all 27, which looks like fault distance costing accuracy. Run the same comparison by rolling-origin cross-validation and the sign reverses: 27 features win by 0.007, and the core set wins only two folds of five. Neither result is significant (p = .95 on the test split, p = .56 across folds), neither confidence interval excludes zero, and the whole effect is 1.3% of the model's own error.

Two protocols disagreeing in sign about an effect smaller than their own noise is what a null result looks like. The defensible statement is that this data set cannot resolve whether fault distance helps — not that it helps, and not that it hurts. Dist_std ranks third of 27 by SHAP attribution, which is a reminder that influence inside a fitted model is a different quantity from a gain in held-out error. Settling it would need more sequences, not a different algorithm.

Against the standard baseline

Test partition, scored against the ETAS magnitude component
ModelRMSESkill vs. ETAS

ETAS is the reference model for Philippine seismicity, so the comparison belongs here. ETAS forecasts the rate of earthquakes, not the magnitude of a particular one, and its magnitude component is the Gutenberg-Richter distribution drawn independently of history. Above M 5.0 that distribution has expectation M0 + 1/(b ln 10), a constant, with b estimated by Aki-Utsu maximum likelihood on the training targets alone. As a magnitude forecaster ETAS is therefore a constant, and it is scored as one rather than waved past: M 5.62, RMSE 0.586.

Extra Trees beats it by 10.5% skill and Random Forest by 5.9%; the LSTM trails it by 4.4%. Fitting a full ETAS space-time intensity would be the right comparison for the occurrence model rather than this one, and this study does not do it.

Whether, not just how large

A second model was built for the other half of the objective: the chance an earthquake of a given size follows within two weeks. A regularised logistic model over eight features beats the historical rate in all three M 5.0 cells, by 6.0%, 5.8% and 2.1% Brier skill at 50, 100 and 200 km. It finds nothing at M 6.0 and there are too few M 7.0 events to score at all, so those cells still report the observed frequency with its margin of error at 95% confidence.

Each radius is a separately trained model fitted on the events inside that radius, and the panel above feeds each one a window computed at its own radius. A 50 km ring holding none of your events falls back to the historical rate and says so rather than borrowing the figure from a wider ring.

The mainshock is the largest event, strictly

A mainshock is by definition the largest earthquake of its sequence. If a preceding event merely ties it, which of the two is the mainshock cannot be decided, so that window is not a foreshock sequence either. An earlier version of this data set broke the rule in 624 of 790 windows, because the observation radius of 100 km is far wider than Maeda's spatial window L, which is 5 km at M 5.0 and 16 km at M 6.0. Earthquakes that declustering had correctly assigned to a different cluster were being swept in as foreshocks, 92% of them from outside that event's own Maeda radius, a median 48 km away.

Those windows are rejected. 166 sequences remain, every foreshock strictly smaller than the mainshock it precedes, by at least 0.1 and a median of 0.50 magnitude units. The rule cost skill: 28.9% on the looser 199-sequence set against 11.4% here. The definition is still the right one to defend, and the smaller number is the one that goes in the paper.

Why the large ones come back small

SHAP, on the M 7.1 the evaluator asked about

SHAP decomposes a single forecast into the training mean plus one signed contribution per feature, and those contributions sum exactly to the forecast. It is the right tool for the question, and the answer is arithmetic rather than opinion.

The base value is M 5.57: what the model says knowing nothing about a sequence. For the M 7.1 in the test partition, all 27 feature contributions together come to plus 0.04. The largest foreshock in that window was M 4.3, below the average for the training set, so the single most influential feature actually pushed the forecast down.

That sequence did not look unusual. Nothing in the fourteen days before a magnitude 7.1 earthquake distinguished it from the ordinary run of magnitude 5.5 sequences, and no change of algorithm can extract a signal from a window that does not contain one. The model has three M 7.0 sequences to learn from in the entire training partition, and the validation partition has none at all.

This is the limit of the method on this catalogue, stated plainly. The panel above reports the ensemble's 90th percentile alongside its mean for the same reason: a mean fitted under squared error cannot reach a tail it has barely seen.

Why the numbers hold

Built so the result survives being checked.

1

Chronological splits

Every validation sequence postdates every training sequence. A shuffled split is the most common way a seismic forecast turns out to be an artefact.

2

Leak-free preprocessing

Imputation, winsorising and scaling are refitted inside each fold from that fold's training rows alone.

3

Naive baselines included

Climatology and persistence are scored alongside every model, so skill is measured against something, not asserted.

4

Cross-language verification

The browser reimplementation is replayed against Python on every held-out sequence, feature by feature and prediction by prediction, and the build fails if any pair disagrees past 1e-5.

5

Fully reproducible

One seed, one command, one file. Re-running the analysis reproduces every number on this page and in the document.

6

Limits published

What the model cannot do is given its own section rather than buried in a footnote.

7

Magnitudes taken, never adjusted

Every magnitude is the value DOST-PHIVOLCS published for that earthquake. None is converted between scales, rescaled or recomputed; where four are reported the first available in a fixed order is adopted and the rest are left alone. The energy feature is derived from the published magnitude by the standard log-energy relation, so it tracks the released energy exactly as the official record documents it.

8

Nothing is uploaded

The models and the catalogue are downloaded to your browser and every forecast is computed there. Sequences you enter, and files you load, never leave your device.

Stated plainly

What this cannot do.

A forecasting paper is only as trustworthy as the list of things it refuses to claim.

Not a prediction system

It does not say when or where

The model estimates the magnitude a mainshock would reach given a foreshock sequence already in progress. It forecasts neither the time nor the location of an earthquake, and nothing here is an early-warning capability.

Foreshocks are rare

Most large events arrive unannounced

Of 3,261 candidate mainshocks, 2,470 had too little catalogued activity in the preceding fortnight to support a forecast, and a further 624 were not strictly the largest event in their own window. 166 remain, and 5 of those reach M 7.0. The method is silent for the great majority of damaging earthquakes.

Occurrence resists modelling

How large is learnable, whether is not

Conditional on a mainshock following, its magnitude is forecast with 11.4% skill. Whether one follows at all is answered only for M 5.0, where the probability model beats the historical rate at all three radii. At M 6.0 it finds no skill and at M 7.0 there are too few events to score, so the honest answer there remains the observed frequency.

Detection, not seismicity

The record grew with the network

Recorded events rise sharply through the 1970s and after, tracking the expansion of the monitoring network rather than a change in the ground. Any temporal trend confounds the two.

One fault layer

Fault distance is only as good as the map

Distances are measured to the active fault lines digitised in QGIS, 1,015 mapped vertices supplied by the authors. A fault absent from that layer is invisible to the model, and the distance is to the nearest vertex rather than to the nearest point on the trace between two of them.

Historical data only

Everything here comes from the PHIVOLCS catalogue

This page and the models behind it are built entirely on the historical earthquake record published by DOST-PHIVOLCS, 1907 to 2026. They can describe only the patterns that record contains. They carry no live seismic feed, no real-time monitoring and no connection to any warning system, and nothing on this page is an official advisory. Those come from PHIVOLCS alone.

The large events are the ones it misses

Above M 7.0 the forecast is about 1.4 too low

Error is not spread evenly. The models are close in the M 5.0 to 5.9 band that supplies four in five training sequences, and fall roughly 0.6 short at M 6.0 to 6.9 and 1.4 short above M 7.0. Three M 7.0 sequences exist in the entire training partition and none in the validation partition. A model fitted under squared error shrinks toward the average it was shown, and this one was shown almost no large earthquakes. The panel reports the ensemble's 90th percentile alongside its mean so the shortfall is visible rather than implied.

False alarms and misses

Both error directions are real and neither is eliminated

The occurrence model beats the historical rate at M 5.0 by between 2 and 6 percent Brier skill, which is a real improvement and a small one. It finds no skill at M 6.0 and cannot be scored at M 7.0. Any claim that this method minimises false alarms, or minimises missed warnings, would be stronger than the measurements support. The confidence intervals on the magnitude forecast overlap the baseline's almost completely.
